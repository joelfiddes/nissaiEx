Purpose. This document is the standing measurement protocol for the Nissai site. It defines what is measured, how, in what units, and against which convention — so that a team member can execute each objective and record results in a form directly comparable with the 2021–2025 record. Logistics, permitting and safety planning are covered separately; only the hazards that bear directly on executing a measurement are reproduced here.

Carry this document to basecamp. Field data sheets follow each SOP and are designed to be completed in pencil.

Hazards that constrain the measurements Rockfall. On 2 Sep 2025 at approx. 15:00 local, two large rockfalls released from the north wall south of basecamp at ~4450 m, in a warming permafrost zone during an exceptionally warm year. Smaller falls were audible throughout the visit — unusual relative to previous years. In 2025 sleeping tents were moved to the moraine on the north side of the valley. Reassess the runout on arrival before siting camp or working below that wall.

Unstable block slope below the ridge loggers. Slope movement has increased as the glacier toe buttressing it melts out. The ridge BLE loggers (WP44 / 47 / 48) were deliberately not accessed in 2025. Do not climb this slope in 2026 — the Geoprecision bedrock logger replaces the need to.

Nissai stream crossing. Discharge rises sharply from midday with glacier melt. The 2023 tree-trunk bridge was destroyed by the spring 2024 floods; a replacement was built to order by a team from Sangvor ahead of the 2025 visit. Confirm bridge status by phone before departure and arrange rebuilding in advance if needed — Sangvor has 4G coverage, Nissai does not. The separate road bridge (built Oct 2022) avoids fording on the drive in.

Critical — SD card removal The sonic anemometer writes at high frequency and the full record exists only on the SD card, not in logger memory. To remove it: press the eject button, which flushes remaining data from memory to disk. Do not pull the card until the LED turns green. This normally takes about 30 seconds. Pulling early truncates or corrupts the year's high-frequency record.

Known faults to diagnose in 2026

Two faults are already known from the telemetry analysis. Both need deliberate, documented diagnosis on site — neither will be solved by a routine service pass.

Fault A — satellite telemetry: assume the remote link is dead Satellite transmission via the AstroNode / Astrocast link first degraded to increasing data gaps, then went to total outage from 15 March 2026 — the second such incident, and by April far the longest. Astrocast could not be reached by email, publishes no phone number and no company status; the portal still loads but carries no new data and will no longer register devices. The vendor's continued operation is in doubt.

Two consequences for the field team. First, the remotely transmitted data is not usable for scientific analysis — the on-site download is the only record, which makes SOP 1 the single most valuable half-day of the campaign. Second, and more important operationally: there has been no remote visibility of station health since March. The station may have been running perfectly, or may have failed months ago. Arrive equipped to find it dead, not merely to service it.
Fault B — snow height sensor (Lufft SHM31) Missing values in the snow-height record. Suspected poor reflectivity of the target surface, or a sensor fault — the analysis could not go further because the sensor request routine in the logger program was written by someone else. Diagnose both possibilities on site, per the procedure below.

Procedure

  1. Approach and document. Photograph the station from a fixed vantage before touching anything — mast lean, snow/ice accretion, animal damage, guy-wire tension, panel soiling.
  2. Diagnose the AstroNode before disturbing anything. Photograph the antenna and its full sky view. Check for rime or ice accretion, physical obstruction, and whether the antenna is still correctly oriented and level — the analysis named all of these as candidate station-side causes. Read the AstroNode's own status indicators and queue/log, and record whether it believes it is transmitting. This is the evidence that separates a station-side fault from a vendor-side outage, and it can only be gathered here. Clear, re-seat and re-aim the antenna as needed; photograph as left.
  3. Diagnose the SHM31. Inspect and clean the optical window. Check the sensor is aimed at nadir and rigidly mounted. Inspect the target surface beneath it for poor reflectivity — dark debris, meltwater, ice glaze, disturbed ground. Take a manual tape measurement from the sensor face to the target surface and record it alongside a simultaneous sensor reading; this offset is what lets the record be diagnosed and corrected later. Note the SHM31 measurement routine as it appears in the logger program.
  4. Record station state. Battery voltage, logger clock vs. GPS time (record the drift before correcting), panel condition, enclosure desiccant colour, evidence of water ingress.
  5. Download logger memory. Collect all logger tables to the field laptop. Verify the file opens and the last record is dated today.
  6. Eject and copy the SD card per the critical note above. Copy to two separate physical media before the card leaves your hand.
  7. Verify before reinstalling. Confirm record count and that the timeseries is continuous — gaps found now can sometimes still be recovered; gaps found in Switzerland cannot.
  8. Reinsert the card, confirm logging resumes, and watch at least one scan complete.
  9. Sensor service. Clean the radiometer domes and the SI-111 window; clear the SHM31 optical path; check the FlowCapt tubes are clear and mounting is rigid; confirm sonic transducer paths are unobstructed.
  10. Timelapse camera. Swap the 256 GB card (a full year needs ~70 GB). Keep PIR trigger OFF — it produced excessive false triggers in 2024. Retain the acquisition schedule adopted in 2024 for valley light conditions; record the exact schedule as set. Replace batteries regardless of indicated level.
  11. Close out. Photograph the station as left. Log every change made in the field sheet — an undocumented change becomes an unexplained step in the timeseries.
Why the sonic record matters The high-frequency sonic data supports the SLF method for deriving latent heat flux from sonic measurements alone, without high-frequency humidity. Applied to the 2021–22 season it gave ~0.73 mm/day of snow sublimation — over 150 mm/yr, a material term in the high-altitude water budget. Combined with FlowCapt drift data it is also the route to a defensible annual solid precipitation total at a site where wind erosion of the snow surface is clearly visible. Losing this record costs more than a year of wind speed.

2Ground temperature network — readout & maintenance

Distributed GST network, 2429–4616 m a.s.l. · BLE loggers (2021–) + Geoprecision bedrock logger (2022–)
Stop — settle this before the campaign, not in the field
The readout app will not install on a modern phone. The SLF-Logger Manager APK targets API level 19 (Android 4.4). Android enforces a minimum installable target API level: Android 14 refuses to install anything targeting below API 23, and Android 15 raises that bar to API 24. On any handset running Android 14 or newer the install fails outright with INSTALL_FAILED_DEPRECATED_SDK_VERSION.
1Take a phone running Android 13 or older. A dedicated older handset kept for this purpose is the robust answer, and it settles every other compatibility question at the same time.
2Sideload over ADB: adb install --bypass-low-target-sdk-block app-release.apk. Works, but needs a cable, USB debugging enabled and platform-tools installed — arrange and test it before departure, not at the trailhead.
Do not factory-reset or replace a working readout phone before this campaign. The block applies only to new installations, so a handset that already has the app keeps it through an OS upgrade. That working phone is campaign-critical equipment.
Location services must be switched ON on the phone — not merely permitted. Android has required location to be enabled for Bluetooth LE scanning since Android 6. With it off the scan returns nothing at all and every logger reads as absent.

Ground surface temperature integrates the surface energy balance, which makes it the cheapest high-value component of a cryosphere monitoring network and the most useful for distributed model validation. Loggers below the 0 °C MAGST line are likely to sit in permafrost terrain. Two sensor types are deployed: SLF-Logger BLE units read over Bluetooth at short range, and one Geoprecision bedrock logger with a radio transmitter, read from up to ~100 m.

The SLF-Logger BLE — specification

Built by SLF Electronics and Mechanics for the permafrost team. Understanding two numbers changes how you search for one and how long you wait: radio range up to 20 m line of sight, and it advertises only once every 10 seconds.

Ageing of the network The Nissai loggers were installed 23–25 September 2021, so in September 2026 they are entering year 5 of a ~8.5-year design life. Battery is not yet the expected failure mode, but the estimate assumes hourly logging and roughly two readouts a year — and cold reduces delivered capacity. Record the reported battery state at every logger this year so that the decline can be tracked before it starts costing records.
Do not access the ridge block slope (WP44 / WP47 / WP48) Movement in this rock slope has increased as the glacier toe buttressing it melts out — flagged in 2024, avoided entirely in 2025. The Geoprecision logger at WP100 can be read by radio from the glacier, which removes the reason to climb it. Treat these three BLE loggers as suspended, not lost. Any decision to resume them is a project decision made before departure, not a judgement call on the slope.

Be patient — the commonest field error is declaring a live logger dead The logger sends an advertising packet only once every 10 seconds. The app's scan window times out after 30 seconds and must be restarted manually. Connecting can take 30 seconds or considerably longer if the advertising packet does not fall inside the scanning window.

A cold, tired person who scans once, sees nothing and moves on will write off a working logger. Restart the scan at least three times, from more than one position, before concluding anything. Conversely, the 20 m range means a buried logger may still answer a scan — so scan before you dig, and treat a radio response as proof of life even when nothing is visible.

Procedure — SLF-Logger BLE

  1. Navigate to the recorded waypoint and scan first, before searching visually. A response tells you the logger is alive and within ~20 m. Restart the scan several times per the warning above, moving position between attempts. Record how many scans you ran before concluding a logger is absent.
  2. Photograph the site as found, including enough surroundings to relocate it if the marker is gone.
  3. Connect: long-press the entry in the logger list (Android). The list shows the last-known name and serial number plus signal strength. Expect to wait.
  4. Read the status screen before doing anything, and write it down. It shows serial number, status, data count, battery %, time of first measurement, Est. Endtime, project / name / location, lat-lng, altitude, interval, accuracy, and — importantly — Phone-Time, Logger-Time and the Difference between them. That Difference is the logger's clock drift over the year, and starting a new measurement overwrites the clock, destroying it. Capture it now or lose it. A status of Interrupted - Err is meaningful: note it verbatim.
  5. Stop the measurement to read it out. Readout only works in the stopped state — press Stop, confirm the prompt, and the readout follows. A full memory can take up to 9 minutes to read and save: stay with the phone, keep it warm, and do not let it lose the connection.
  6. Heed the warning dialog. If the app says "It seems you have read the data some time ago. Are you sure you have read it? On start the old data will be erased!" — do not tap through it. Starting a new measurement without reading out first erases the year. This is the one irreversible mistake available at a GST site.
  7. Verify on screen in the DataViewer before leaving: the record should span the full year with no long flat section. A flatlined trace usually means the logger became detached, buried or exposed — diagnosable now, never later.
  8. Restart logging. Set the project settings and position (the GPS button, or Fix to take the phone's coordinates; altitude must be entered separately). Note the Est. Endtime the app predicts and the reason it gives — memory full or battery empty. That single figure tells you whether this logger will survive to the next visit.
  9. Confirm the status reads Measuring (or Delayed Start) before disconnecting.
  10. Disconnect deliberately with the Return key. The connection persists until manually dropped and holds the screen bright the whole time — walking away with it connected drains both the phone and the logger.
  11. Record substrate (rock / sediment / debris), aspect and slope, and any change since installation — burial, exposure, vegetation, disturbance.
  12. If replacing a logger, install within 10 m of the original, record the new position, and clearly log both the retired and the new ID.
Clock and timezone — record what you set On the Windows version, starting a measurement overwrites the logger clock with PC time (UTC+1), not local Tajikistan time. Whatever device is used, write down the timezone the logger clock was actually set to on the field sheet. An unrecorded 4-hour offset is invisible in the data and corrupts every diurnal analysis built on it.

The Geoprecision bedrock logger (WP100)

A wire probe inserted into a 6 mm hole about 10 cm deep in exposed bedrock at 4416 m. Bedrock responds to atmospheric forcing far more directly than debris, which makes this the interpretive anchor for the whole debris-logger network — and, since the ridge BLE loggers are suspended, the only sensor still reporting from that slope.

The manufacturer's instruction is blunt: set the PC clock to UTC, accurate to the second, before connecting — and disable automatic daylight-saving adjustment, which otherwise introduces a silent step in the record.

Note that this is the opposite convention to the SLF BLE loggers, whose clock follows whatever device reads them. Two logger families, two clock conventions, on the same trip. Record which was used for each — an unlabelled offset is invisible in the data and corrupts every diurnal analysis downstream.

Procedure — Geoprecision bedrock logger

  1. Set and verify the laptop clock (UTC, DST off) before leaving camp. Plug in the 433 MHz dongle and allow about a minute before launching the software.
  2. Approach to within radio range from the glacier — read successfully from about 100 m in 2025. Do not approach the slope itself.
  3. Connect using access code DE10 and confirm the logger ID matches A542D3.
  4. Use "Load to Disk" and choose a FULL upload, not Incremental. Incremental fetches only new data; Full re-reads everything. Memory holds decades, the transfer costs minutes, and a full copy makes this download self-contained rather than dependent on every previous one being intact.
  5. Save both formats — the compressed .G2D / .F2B file and the expanded CSV. The compressed file is the faithful original; the CSV is what everyone will actually open.
  6. Verify before disconnecting: the record ends today and begins where the 2025 download ended. Check the peak summer values are plausible — this logger reached 30 °C in summer 2025, so a suspiciously flat or clipped trace is worth catching now.
  7. Note the battery level. If a battery change is ever attempted, save the data to the PC first and re-verify every logger setting afterwards — settings can be lost with the power.
  8. If it will not transmit, log that and stop. Do not attempt physical access to the block slope under any circumstances.
Two dongles, one laptop — check the kit before departure The two logger families need two different, non-interchangeable USB dongles: a BLED112 (Bluetooth LE) for the SLF loggers if reading them from Windows, and a 433 MHz GeoPrecision dongle for the bedrock logger. Neither has a substitute, both are small enough to be forgotten, and each renders an entire objective impossible if left behind. Pack them together, with their drivers installed and tested on the field laptop, and carry a spare of the 433 MHz dongle if one exists — it is the only route to WP100.
Context for interpretation The WP100 record runs continuously from September 2022 and reached a peak surface temperature of 30 °C in summer 2025. Both the record temperature and the rockfall activity that season sit in the same warming-permafrost picture. When plotting MAGST against elevation on return, carry the zero line — loggers below it indicate probable permafrost terrain.

3Catchment stream gauge — re-establishment & gauging

Nissai stream · monitoring interrupted since spring 2024 · re-establishment is a carried-over priority
Standing gap in the record Both stream gauges installed in 2022 were lost, presumed to the destructive spring 2024 floods — only the U-bracket remained bolted to the rock. The flood damage was evident along the whole drive to Sangvor and within the Nissai catchment itself. Re-establishment and discharge gauging were named key priorities for 2025 and remain the priority for 2026. Confirm the current on-the-ground status before finalising the equipment list, since the 2025 outcome for this objective is not written up in the field report.

Approach agreed in 2024

The new road bridge (built Oct 2022) offers a stable cross-section on gabioned foundations — a far better mounting than the previous bank site. The identified approach is a lidar-based stage monitoring system mounted on the bridge, measuring downward to the water surface from a fixed, surveyed reference.

Procedure

  1. Site assessment. Photograph the bridge and cross-section. Confirm the gabion foundations are intact after the most recent spring melt. Check the surviving U-bracket at the old site and decide whether to recover or abandon it.
  2. Mount the stage sensor rigidly to the bridge structure, sighting a section of water surface that stays wetted at low flow and is clear of turbulence, bridge shadow and debris rafts.
  3. Survey the datum. Measure and record the vertical offset from the sensor face to a permanent, photographed benchmark on the bridge, plus the offset to the water surface at the time of install. Without this the stage record cannot be re-levelled after any future remount, and the series is not continuable.
  4. Install a staff gauge or painted reference readable from the bank, so any visitor can log a manual stage reading. Photograph it with the water level visible.
  5. Barometric compensation. If using a pressure-based backup, site the on-land barometer where construction cannot reach it — the 2023 on-land HOBO was destroyed by the road bridge works. Record its position and height precisely.
  6. Survey the cross-section: width, and depth at fixed intervals across the section, referenced to the same benchmark.
  7. Gauge discharge at least twice at contrasting flows (e.g. early morning low flow and afternoon peak) to begin a rating curve. Record the method, the measured discharge, the simultaneous stage, and the time of each.
  8. Set logging interval and clock, record both, and confirm the first records are being written before leaving the site.
Timing note Sampling both a morning and an afternoon flow on the same day exploits the strong diurnal glacier-melt signal to get two well-separated rating points at no extra travel cost. The same diurnal rise is what makes the stream unsafe to cross from midday onward — plan the two gaugings from the bridge, not from a wade.

4Ablation stake network — read out & redrill

Nissai Glacier · 7 ablation stakes (4068–4387 m) + 1 accumulation stake · annual glaciological mass balance

Measurement convention — read this before measuring anything

The archive uses three quantities per stake. Getting these the right way round is the single most important thing on the glacier:

QuantityDefinition
Stake lengthTotal physical length of the stake assembly, cm. Segments are 2 m, joined by steel chains and screws.
Stake in iceLength below the ice surface immediately after drilling, cm. At full insertion this equals the stake length and emergence is zero.
Stake measuredEmergent length above the ice surface at the time of the visit, cm.

Annual ablation at a stake is therefore:

Calculation ablation (cm ice equiv.) = emergentthis year − emergentlast year, where emergentlast year = stake length − stake in ice.
b (m w.e.) = − ablation (cm i.e.) × 0.9 / 100, using ρice = 900 kg m−3.

Worked example from 2024–25, stake 6: length 400, in ice 2024 = 228 → emergent 2024 = 172 cm. Measured 2025 = 378 cm. Ablation = 378 − 172 = 206 cm i.e. → b = −1.854 m w.e. ✓

Plan for full melt-out Four of seven stakes are projected to be within ~120 cm of melting out. In 2025 stake 1 was found fully melted out and its mass balance had to be estimated from the linear relationship across stakes 2–6 — a real loss of an independent observation at the most sensitive elevation. Carry enough 2 m segments and chain to rebuild every stake to full length, and prioritise the lowest stakes early in the day in case time runs short.

Procedure

  1. Locate and identify. Navigate to the 2025 waypoint. Read the physical label on the stake and record it verbatim, even if it disagrees with the expected label.
  2. Photograph as found, with a person or pole for scale, before touching the stake.
  3. Measure emergent length to the ice surface. Where the stake sits in a melt cup or on a pedestal, measure to the general surrounding ice surface, not to the local depression, and note that a correction was applied.
  4. Record tilt. If the stake leans, measure the lean or record the vertical height separately from the along-stake length. An uncorrected lean is a systematic bias in ablation.
  5. Record the position as a new GPS waypoint — stakes move with the ice, so the 2026 coordinate is a measurement in its own right.
  6. Drill the new stake. Mechanical drill with the 6 m Kovacs ice auger in 1 m sections. Drill the precise full stake length into the ice so that emergence at installation is zero, or record the exact insertion depth if it is not.
  7. Where the old stake still holds, install the new stake alongside and measure both. Paired side-by-side measurements were highly consistent in 2024 and are the only way to catch a bad reading — the 2023 NS7 measurement was identified as erroneous exactly this way.
  8. Photograph as left, and record the new label, length, and in-ice depth on the sheet before moving to the next stake.

Connecting to the CR350

Physically trivial; the software side must be set up on the actual Toughbook before departure. Driver installation wants administrator rights and sometimes an internet connection — neither exists at 4000 m.

PointDetail
CableUSB-C at the logger. Check the Toughbook's own ports — rugged laptops are frequently USB-A only, in which case you need an A-to-C cable, not the C-to-C that comes to mind. Carry two; they weigh nothing.
PowerThe USB port supplies 5 V and powers the logger for communications. You can therefore connect and download even if the station battery is flat — a download path, not a repair. Normal field operation still needs 12 V.
SoftwareLoggerNet, PC400 or RTDAQ. If the Toughbook already runs LoggerNet for the AWS CR1000X, the software is there — you are only adding a second station. The USB driver is device-family specific and may still need installing.
DriverFrom within the support software: Install USB Driver → select the logger → follow prompts. It then enumerates as a COM port shown as CR350 series (COMn).
Device typeIn the EZSetup wizard, select "CR300 Series" — there is no "CR350" entry in the list. The CR350 belongs to the CR300 series. Someone hunting for "CR350" will conclude the software is wrong and waste the visit.
Connection typeDirect Connect, then choose the COM port.
SettingsAll defaults: PakBus address 1 · default baud · COM port delay 0 · extra response time 0 · max time on-line 0 · neighbour PakBus address 0 · security code 0 · encryption key blank.
There is no SD card — and the memory may have wrapped The CR350 has no microSD slot and no CRD: drive. The eject-button-and-green-LED ritual from the AWS belongs to the CR1000X and does not apply here — do not go looking for a card. Data lives in internal serial flash, of which roughly 50 MB is available for data storage.

That capacity is small, and Campbell loggers default to ring memory: once full, new records overwrite the oldest. After a year unattended, the record may not reach back to installation. Check the timestamp of the first record, not only the last, and note it on the field sheet — it tells you whether the year is complete or whether the table wrapped. If the program used FillStop instead, logging will have stopped when the table filled, which looks like a dead station but is not.

If a security code or PakBus encryption key was set in 2025, the defaults above will fail and the logger will simply not respond — in the field that is indistinguishable from dead hardware. Get both from Evan / Stefan / Ines before departure.
Fallback — USB host port The CR350 has a second, host USB port that takes a FAT32-formatted thumb drive, written by the TableFile() instruction. This only helps if the running program was written to use it, so check the program rather than plan around it. If a drive is found attached: do not pull it while the power LED is flashing red — that indicates read/write activity and removing it can corrupt the drive.

On arrival — decide which case you are in, then follow that branch

Photograph the station as found before anything else, then classify. In all three cases, attempt the download first — data recovery precedes repair, because a repair can destroy the evidence of what failed.

CaseWhat you findAction
AStanding and stableDownload via Toughbook. Service, confirm logging resumes, and leave in place. Record the new depth register.
BStanding but loose or leaning — mast melted out of its hole by a season of ablationDownload first. Then stabilise the station: redrill the mast to a proper depth and re-erect it vertical. Record the new emergent height and full depth register — the sensors are now at different depths than in 2025, and only the on-site measurement can establish that.
CCollapsed or destroyedAttempt data recovery from the logger regardless of its physical state. Then bring the station down off the glacier — do not leave debris or equipment on the ice. Photograph the failure mode thoroughly before disturbing it; that is the only evidence of why it failed.
Plan for case C Case C is considered a realistic outcome, not a remote one. That has two practical consequences: budget the load capacity to carry the station out (mast sections, logger, battery, sensors), and carry basic tools for dismantling a partly ice-encased structure. Confirm before departure whether a replacement installation is intended for 2026 — if so, the replacement hardware has to be carried in on the same trip.

Procedure

  1. Locate and photograph as found — wide context and close detail. Record how much mast is emergent and whether the station is still vertical.
  2. Download the record via USB-C from the Toughbook. Verify on screen that it is continuous from installation to today, and copy to two media before leaving the site.
  3. Measure emergent mast height and surface lowering. A year of ablation at this elevation is of the order of 2–3 m, so the ice surface will have dropped substantially relative to the station. Measured against the installation record, this is what converts fixed sensor spacing into actual depths below the current surface — and it cannot be reconstructed later.
  4. Check every channel returns plausible values. Note any that have failed, flatlined, or are now reading air rather than ice because the surface dropped past them.
  5. Record battery state and replace if there is any doubt about surviving another year.
  6. Re-survey the position — the station moves with the ice. Record a new waypoint and the displacement from the 2025 coordinates.
  7. Follow the case A / B / C action from the table above.
  8. Photograph as left and write down every change, including the full new depth register if the mast was redrilled.

6Data handling, end-of-day routine & close-out

The rules that keep a hard-won year of data from being lost on the walk out

Every evening at camp

  1. Copy the day's downloads to two physically separate media, carried by two different people. One SD card in one bag is a single point of failure for an entire year of record.
  2. Export the day's GPS track and waypoints to GPX and copy alongside the data.
  3. Transcribe the field sheets into the digital metadata tables the same evening, while the shorthand is still legible and the memory is fresh. Keep the paper sheets — they are the primary record.
  4. Offload and rename photographs against the instrument or stake they document.
  5. Update the running action list — anything found broken, missing, ambiguous or deferred. This list becomes the next campaign's equipment order.
